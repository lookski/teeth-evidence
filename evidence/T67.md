# T67. Charcoal whitening toothpaste: ΔE 3.3 in every group, no advantage, no extra abrasion

> Hierarchy: [← README](../README.md) · [Topic overview 06-hot-topics](../topics/06-hot-topics.md) · `T67`

## Bottom line

An in vitro study stained **30 premolars** in coffee for 5 days, then brushed them **2000 times**
(equivalent to 3 times a day for 1.5 months) with three toothpastes — two charcoal products
(Benecer, Beverly) and Colgate. All three produced **ΔE = 3.3, within the acceptable range**, and
there was **no significant difference in abrasion (P>0.05) or colour change (P=0.884)** among them.
So charcoal whitening toothpaste worked no better than an ordinary toothpaste, and was not more
abrasive either — it is simply a marketing story priced at a premium.

## Study designs and populations

- Design: in vitro brushing-machine experiment
- Specimens: **30 premolars**, polished, sectioned, mounted; stained 5 days in coffee at 37 C
- Groups: 3 groups of 10, one per toothpaste (Benecer, Beverly, Colgate)
- Brushing protocol: **2000 strokes** in a brushing machine = 3 times/day for **1.5 months**
- Slurry: 20 g toothpaste + 40 ml distilled water
- Outcomes: surface profile change (abrasion), colour change ΔE

## Key numbers (verbatim from abstract)

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Surface profile change (all three pastes) | significant, **P = 0.0001** |
| **ΔE, all groups** | **3.3** (within the acceptable range; 95% CI) |
| Abrasion difference between pastes | **not significant (P>0.05)** |
| Colour change difference between pastes | **not significant (P = 0.884)** |
| Specimens | 30 premolars |
| Brushing | 2000 strokes (~1.5 months of 3x/day) |

## Dose-response and practical interpretation

- The headline number is the null: **ΔE identical at 3.3 in all three groups**, with P=0.884
- ΔE 3.3 sits within the "acceptable" perceptual range — meaning the stain removal was modest for
  everyone, not dramatic for charcoal
- Importantly, charcoal was **not more abrasive** here, which contradicts a common assumption that
  charcoal must be damaging because it is "gritty". Abrasivity depends on the paste formulation,
  not the colour of the ingredient
- Practical: charcoal toothpaste is a premium-priced ordinary paste. If you want real whitening,
  peroxide-based products or professional bleaching have the evidence (see T69); if you want
  stain control, any acceptable-abrasivity paste plus good brushing works

## Strengths and limitations

- Strengths: real human teeth (not acrylic); a brushing protocol calibrated to a realistic time
  equivalent (1.5 months of 3x/day); three pastes compared including a mainstream control;
  reports both efficacy (ΔE) and safety (abrasion) endpoints
- Limitations: **in vitro** with a coffee staining model only (no tea, wine, chlorhexidine stains);
  1.5-month equivalent is short for cumulative whitening; ΔE reported as one value for all groups
  rather than per group, so between-group variance is 以原文为准; only two charcoal brands tested;
  no clinical shade assessment
- This is the cleanest single study for the "charcoal is overhyped" claim, but it is in vitro

## Consistency with other evidence

- Consistent with T66 (head-to-head of six technologies: charcoal was **not** among the best
  performers; blue covarine and peroxide led)
- **Tension** with T68, where a 1%-charcoal paste beat a regular toothpaste on ΔE (+17.7%, P=0.023).
  The two charcoal studies disagree; plausible reasons: different charcoal concentrations, different
  staining protocols, different brushing simulation lengths. Present both, do not cherry-pick
- Consistent with T40's theme that paste abrasivity, not ingredient marketing, drives wear risk
