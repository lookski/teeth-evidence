# T68. 1% charcoal paste does beat regular toothpaste (ΔE +17.7%), but the literature disagrees

> Hierarchy: [← README](../README.md) · [Topic overview 06-hot-topics](../topics/06-hot-topics.md) · `T68`

## Bottom line

An in vitro study on **40 human canines** stained with coffee tested a **1% charcoal whitening
toothpaste**, alone and combined with a charcoal mouthwash, over a simulated **90-day** brushing
period. The whitening toothpaste increased **ΔE by 40.5% compared to the water-brushing control
(P<0.001)** and by **17.7% compared to a regular toothpaste (P=0.023)**. This is the **only**
charcoal study in this round that shows a genuine advantage — and it directly contradicts T67,
where charcoal was indistinguishable from ordinary paste.

## Study designs and populations

- Design: in vitro enamel colour-change experiment
- Specimens: **40 human canines**, stained with coffee
- Groups (n=10 each):
  1. Control — toothbrushing with **deionized water**
  2. **Regular toothpaste**
  3. Whitening toothpaste with **1% charcoal**
  4. Same whitening toothpaste **plus mouthwash** (1% charcoal + 0.5% H2O2)
- Simulation period: brushing corresponding to a **90-day** period
- Outcomes: colour change (ΔE) and enamel surface morphology alterations

## Key numbers (verbatim from abstract)

| Comparison | Result |
|---|---|
| **1% charcoal paste vs water control** | ΔE **+40.5%**, **P<0.001** |
| **1% charcoal paste vs regular toothpaste** | ΔE **+17.7%**, **P=0.023** |
| Charcoal paste + charcoal/H2O2 mouthwash | tested as group 4; abstract reports the paste comparisons above |

## Dose-response and practical interpretation

- The two comparisons tell different stories: vs **water** the effect is large (+40.5%), vs
  **regular toothpaste** it shrinks to +17.7% — i.e. most of the "whitening" is just brushing
- +17.7% ΔE is a real but modest increment; it is not the dramatic before/after sold in advertising
- The 90-day simulated period is the longest in this round, which is why this study carries weight
  despite being in vitro
- Practical: charcoal may add a small whitening increment over ordinary paste, but the effect is
  formulation-dependent (this was a **1%** charcoal product). Given T67's null result and T66's
  ranking, charcoal is not a reliable category recommendation
- Safety note: this study also examined **surface morphology**, i.e. whether the paste roughens
  enamel — 以原文为准 for the direction of that finding

## Strengths and limitations

- Strengths: human teeth (not acrylic or bovine); long simulated period (90 days); graded control
  ladder (water -> regular paste -> charcoal -> charcoal+mouthwash) lets the reader separate
  "brushing effect" from "charcoal effect"
- Limitations: **in vitro**; single 1% charcoal product; coffee-only staining model; n=10 per group;
  the abstract does not report absolute ΔE values or the surface-morphology outcome, both 以原文为准
- Directly conflicts with T67 (charcoal = regular paste, P=0.884) — the discrepancy is the finding

## Consistency with other evidence

- **Conflicts** with T67 (no difference, P=0.884) and with T66 (charcoal not among the best of six
  technologies). Two of three studies say charcoal adds nothing
- Consistent with T69's broader picture: true whitening comes from **peroxide bleaching**, not from
  abrasive or adsorptive ingredients; charcoal's small effect is likely mechanical stain removal
- The honest synthesis: charcoal is at best a mild abrasive stain remover, not a bleaching agent,
  and results vary by formulation
