# T89. Periodontology 2000: non-shedding tooth surfaces build complex polymicrobial biofilms

> Hierarchy: [← README](../README.md) · [Topic overview 06-hot-topics](../topics/06-hot-topics.md) · `T89`

## Bottom line

A Periodontology 2000 review frames oral health and disease as reflecting "the compositional and
functional capacities of, as well as the **interspecies interactions** within, the oral microbiota".
Its key structural point: "the **nonshedding dental surfaces** facilitate the development of highly
complex **polymicrobial biofilm** communities", and the mouth faces "near-constant environmental
challenges, including those from **host diet, salivary flow, masticatory forces, and introduction of
exogenous microbes**". Composition is shaped across life by **host genetics, maternal transmission**,
and environmental factors (**diet, oral hygiene practice, medications, systemic factors**) — a dynamic
ecosystem that presents "opportunities for **oral microbial dysbiosis** and the development of dental
and periodontal diseases".

## Study designs and populations

- Design: **narrative review** (Periodontology 2000, 2021)
- Central concept 1: **non-shedding dental surfaces** as the substrate that allows complex biofilm to
  mature (unlike mucosal surfaces, which shed)
- Central concept 2: **interspecies interactions** — cumulatively the biofilm's activities matter more
  than the list of species
- Environmental challenges named: **host diet, salivary flow, masticatory forces, exogenous microbes**
- Lifetime determinants: **host genetics, maternal transmission, dietary habits, oral hygiene
  practice, medications, systemic factors**
- Disease link: **dysbiosis -> dental and periodontal diseases**
- Methods mentioned: **in vitro and culture-independent approaches**

## Dose-response and practical interpretation

- The **non-shedding surface** insight is the mechanistic reason brushing works: because enamel does
  not shed, biofilm accumulates and matures into a polymicrobial community; remove it mechanically and
  you reset the clock. This is the biological justification for T18's plaque-control programme and for
  T82/T83's interdental tools
- **Salivary flow** appears again as a challenge input (consistent with T86: athletes with flow <= 1
  ml/min had more erosive lesions; and T39's dry-mouth cluster)
- "Interspecies interactions" explains why single-species probiotics or single-agent rinses rarely
  transform a mature biofilm — the community, not one organism, is the target
- Practical: the levers this review names are mostly the ones you control — **mechanical removal,
  diet, saliva, medication review**. No microbiome product appears

## Strengths and limitations

- Strengths: written for a periodontal audience, so it connects ecology to clinical disease; names
  concrete ecological drivers (shear forces, shedding, saliva) rather than vague "balance"; current
  (2021)
- Limitations: **narrative review, no numbers**; abstract stops before presenting intervention data;
  ecological framing is not itself evidence that modulating the microbiome improves outcomes;
  cannot be cited quantitatively
- Conceptual scaffolding for the project's mechanical-cleaning entries

## Consistency with other evidence

- Consistent with T87 and T88 (the other two microbiome reviews): all three are ecological, all three
  avoid quantitative claims in their abstracts
- Consistent with T18 (long-term plaque-control programme) — the non-shedding surface argument is why
  sustained mechanical removal works
- Consistent with T82/T83 (interdental brushes remove more approximal plaque than floss) — site-specific
  biofilm disruption
- Consistent with T86 (salivary flow as erosion risk) and T39 (dry mouth)
- Caution from T84: ecological plausibility does not rescue confounded systemic associations
