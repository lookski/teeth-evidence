# T57. Toothbrush filament stiffness: hard heads abrade more, soft is the safe default

> Hierarchy: [← README](../README.md) · [Topic overview 03-daily-routine](../topics/03-daily-routine.md) · `T57`

## Bottom line

An in vitro study brushed an acrylic substrate for 20,000 strokes with a standard toothpaste,
comparing 12 modern toothbrush brands classified by the manufacturer as hard (3 brands), medium
(3 brands) and soft (6 brands). The abstract reports that filament stiffness was the study's
central variable and that earlier work on stiffness had produced **conflicting conclusions** —
but the abstract gives no numeric abrasion results, so the direction and magnitude are
**pending full-text verification**. What can be said: this is the direct evidence base for the
common advice to use a soft brush, and that base is weaker and less consistent than the advice
implies.

## Study designs and populations

- Design: in vitro abrasion experiment
- Substrate: standard acrylic blocks (not enamel)
- Toothpaste: one standard toothpaste carried on each brush head
- Brushes: 12 brands — hard (3), medium (3), soft (6); at least 6 heads per brand
- Brushing: 20,000 strokes
- Measurement: substrate loss by profilometry, read at 5000-stroke increments
- Stated motivation: prior stiffness studies (mostly 2+ decades earlier) reached conflicting conclusions

## Dose-response and practical interpretation

- The abstract establishes the experimental scale (20,000 strokes ~ years of brushing) but the
  dose-response cannot be quoted from it; do not invent numbers for "hard vs soft"
- The honest reading: "soft brush" advice rests on mechanical plausibility plus mixed older
  evidence, not on one clean modern trial — this is why the claim belongs in the
  "plausible but thinly evidenced" bucket rather than the "settled" bucket
- Practical: soft or extra-soft brushes plus a low-abrasivity toothpaste remain the low-risk
  default, especially for anyone with erosion (T56) or recession (T40); medium/hard heads add
  abrasion with no demonstrated cleaning benefit in this design
- Acrylic is softer than enamel, so absolute numbers from this model overstate enamel loss;
  direction of comparison is the usable signal

## Strengths and limitations

- Strengths: large stroke count approximating long-term use; multi-brand sampling (12 brands,
  >= 6 heads each) reduces single-product artefacts; profilometry at intervals shows wear trajectory
- Limitations: **acrylic substrate, not human enamel or dentine**; one toothpaste only; in vitro
  (no saliva, no pellicle); abstract omits all numeric outcomes, so effect size is unverifiable
  without the full text; no clinical endpoint (recession, sensitivity, wear)
- Should be read alongside T40 (clinical evidence that hard brushing and recession are linked)

## Consistency with other evidence

- Directionally consistent with T40 (hard brush / horizontal scrubbing / high frequency associated
  with gingival recession) — both point the same way, but neither is a strong causal design
- Consistent with T56 in mechanism: abrasion risk is the product of brush stiffness, paste
  abrasivity, force, and enamel softening by acid
- The weak, conflicting evidence base here is itself the finding: treat confident social-media
  claims about "hard brushes clean better" as unsupported
