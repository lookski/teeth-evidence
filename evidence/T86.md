# T86. Dental erosive wear in fit young adults: 64% vs 20%, and low salivary flow tracks the lesions

> Hierarchy: [← README](../README.md) · [Topic overview 03-daily-routine](../topics/03-daily-routine.md) · `T86`

## Bottom line

**220 participants** aged **18-32** from a fitness centre were examined with the **VEDE** erosive wear
system: dental erosive wear was registered in **64% of the exercising participants** versus **20% of
the non-exercising comparison group**. One quarter of participants had wear **into dentine**,
significantly more in **males than females (p = 0.047)**. More participants with erosive wear had
**decreased salivary flow during exercise** than the non-erosion group (**p < 0.01**), and more than
one third had a **stimulated salivary flow rate <= 1 ml/min**, which was associated with more erosive
lesions (**p < 0.01**).

## Study designs and populations

- Design: cross-sectional comparison (exercising group vs non-exercising comparison group)
- Exercising group: young members (age range **18-32 years**) of a fitness centre, healthy, training
  hard **at least twice a week**
- Comparison group: **non-exercising**, selected from an ongoing study among **18-year-olds**
- Sample: **220** participants accepted intraoral examination and completed a questionnaire;
  **70** of the exercising participants provided saliva samples
- Examination: **VEDE** (tested erosive wear system); at the fitness centre or a dental clinic
  (comparison group)
- Saliva: **unstimulated and stimulated**, sampled **before and after exercise**
- Index surfaces: occlusal of first molars (both jaws); labial and palatal of upper incisors and canines

## Dose-response and practical interpretation

- The **64% vs 20%** contrast is the headline: erosive wear is three times more common in hard-training
  young adults than in their non-exercising peers
- The salivary mechanism is coherent: hard exercise reduces stimulated salivary flow, and lower flow
  means less dilution and less remineralisation of acid-exposed enamel. **>1/3 with flow <= 1 ml/min**
  and more lesions in that group is the dose-response supporting it
- Site pattern is clinically recognisable: **upper central incisors (labial)** for enamel, **lower
  first molars (occlusal)** for dentine — the classic erosion distribution
- Practical for athletes: sports drinks and citrus are the usual acid source; swish with water, **do
  not brush immediately** (see T56), and consider fluoride; if you train hard and have dry mouth,
  you are in the high-risk group
- Note: cross-sectional, so "exercise causes erosion" is not established — diet and lifestyle in
  fitness-centre members differ in many ways

## Strengths and limitations

- Strengths: use of the validated **VEDE** index; a defined comparison group; objective salivary
  measurement **before and after exercise**; site-specific lesion reporting; reasonable sample (220)
- Limitations: **cross-sectional** — cannot separate exercise from diet, sports drinks, or
  fitness-centre lifestyle; comparison group was **18-year-olds** while exercisers spanned 18-32, so
  age is confounded with exposure (the authors note wear was more common in the older age group);
  only 70 gave saliva; single region (Oslo, Norway); self-selected fitness-centre volunteers
- The age mismatch is the most important flaw: older participants have had more years to accumulate wear

## Consistency with other evidence

- Consistent with T56 (acid softening plus immediate brushing increases wear; waiting 1 h reduces it)
  — the mechanistic pair for this entry
- Consistent with T27 (erosion: fluoride cannot stop pH<3 attack) — fluoride helps but does not
  neutralise strong acid
- Consistent with T39's cluster on dry mouth as an erosion/caries risk factor — salivary flow is the
  common thread
- Adds an athlete-specific angle not covered elsewhere in this project
