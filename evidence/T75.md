# T75. Antibacterial mouthwash raises systolic blood pressure 2.3 mmHg (randomised crossover)

> Hierarchy: [← README](../README.md) · [Topic overview 06-hot-topics](../topics/06-hot-topics.md) · `T75`

## Bottom line

In a randomised controlled crossover trial in **15 treated hypertensive men and women** (mean age
65), **3-day use of antibacterial mouthwash** versus water control reduced oral nitrate-to-nitrite
reduction (**P = 0.02**), lowered salivary nitrite (**P = 0.01**), raised salivary nitrate
(**P < 0.001**), and **raised systolic blood pressure by 2.3 mmHg (95% CI 0.5 to 4.0, P = 0.01)**,
with no effect on diastolic pressure (P = 0.4) or plasma cGMP (P = 0.7), and only a trend toward
lower plasma nitrite (P = 0.09). A **randomised** signal for harm that does not depend on
confounding.

## Study designs and populations

- Design: **randomised controlled crossover trial**
- Sample: **15** treated hypertensive men and women, **mean age 65**
- Intervention: **3 days** of antibacterial mouthwash use
- Control: water
- Outcomes: oral nitrate-to-nitrite reduction; salivary and plasma nitrate and nitrite; plasma cyclic
  guanosine monophosphate (cGMP); systolic and diastolic blood pressure

## Key numbers (verbatim from abstract)

| Outcome (mouthwash vs water) | Result |
|---|---|
| Oral nitrate-to-nitrite reduction | **decreased, P = 0.02** |
| Salivary nitrite | **decreased, P = 0.01** |
| Salivary nitrate | **increased, P < 0.001** |
| Plasma nitrite | trend toward decrease, **P = 0.09** |
| **Systolic blood pressure** | **+2.3 mmHg (95% CI 0.5, 4.0), P = 0.01** |
| Diastolic blood pressure | no effect, **P = 0.4** |
| Plasma cGMP | no effect, **P = 0.7** |

## Dose-response and practical interpretation

- **Mechanism is clear and coherent**: oral bacteria reduce dietary nitrate to nitrite, which is
  converted to vasodilatory nitric oxide. Antibacterial mouthwash kills those bacteria, so nitrate
  reduction falls, nitrite falls, and blood pressure rises
- The chain checks out internally: salivary nitrate goes **up** (substrate accumulates) while
  salivary nitrite goes **down** (product not formed) — exactly what blocking the bacteria predicts
- **Only 3 days** of use was enough to move systolic pressure 2.3 mmHg. In a hypertensive population,
  sustained shifts of this size matter at population level (the same magnitude that dietary salt
  interventions fight for)
- Practical: people with hypertension, especially those relying on dietary nitrate (beetroot juice,
  leafy greens) for blood pressure, should consider **alcohol-free, non-antiseptic** rinses, or use
  antiseptic rinses sparingly
- Do not extrapolate to cosmetic/fluoride rinses — this was an antibacterial agent specifically

## Strengths and limitations

- Strengths: **randomised crossover** — the strongest design in this mouthwash cluster and immune to
  the confounding that undermines T72/T73; a mechanistic chain measured at multiple points (nitrate,
  nitrite, cGMP, BP); both salivary and plasma compartments sampled
- Limitations: **n = 15**, all treated hypertensives (not generalisable to normotensives); only
  **3 days** — no data on whether the BP rise persists, adapts, or reverses; plasma cGMP unchanged
  weakens the nitric-oxide pathway claim; single antibacterial product; abstract does not report the
  absolute BP values, only the difference; unblinded control of water is perceptibly different
- Small and short, but causally designed — which is exactly why it is worth citing

## Consistency with other evidence

- Consistent with T76 (antiseptic mouthwash abolished the gastroprotective and blood-pressure-lowering
  effects of dietary nitrate in rats) — same mechanism, independent model
- **Independent of** T72/T73 (mouthwash and oral cancer): different endpoint, different mechanism
  (nitric oxide depletion vs acetaldehyde), and unlike those, this one is randomised
- The cluster's synthesis: mouthwash has two credible harm signals — cancer association (observational,
  confounded, heavy use) and blood-pressure elevation (randomised, small, short). Neither justifies
  avoiding mouthwash entirely; both justify preferring alcohol-free non-antiseptic rinses for routine use
