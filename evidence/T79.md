# T79. Baking-soda dentifrice systematic review: better than placebo, unproven against fluoride paste

> Hierarchy: [← README](../README.md) · [Topic overview 06-hot-topics](../topics/06-hot-topics.md) · `T79`

## Bottom line

A systematic review of **21 eligible publications** (43 comparisons: 23 single-use, 20 with
follow-up) found baking-soda dentifrice significantly better than negative-control dentifrices on
plaque (**DiffM -0.20, 95% CI -0.27 to -0.12, P<0.0001**) and than positive-control dentifrices
(**DiffM -0.18, 95% CI -0.24 to -0.12, P<0.0001**) in single-brushing experiments. In follow-up
designs, the advantage held only versus negative control (**DiffM -0.19, 95% CI -0.34 to -0.04,
P=0.01**). Gingival bleeding also improved versus negative control (**DiffM -0.08, P=0.02** and
**-0.13, P<0.001**). So: real but small plaque benefit over placebo; the long-term advantage over
established pastes is not established.

## Study designs and populations

- Design: systematic review of **randomized controlled clinical trials**
- Population: healthy participants aged **18 years or older**
- Search yield: **21 eligible publications**, yielding **43 comparisons**
- Comparison types: **23 single-use designs** (single brushing), **20 evaluations with follow-up**
- Comparators: negative-control dentifrices (no active) and positive-control dentifrices (established
  actives, e.g. fluoride)
- Outcomes: plaque scores, gingival bleeding indices

## Dose-response and practical interpretation

- The pattern is instructive: the advantage over **positive controls** exists in single-use
  experiments (-0.18) but is **not confirmed in follow-up designs**. That is the classic signature of
  a short-term cleaning boost that does not translate into sustained benefit
- Effect sizes (-0.08 to -0.20 on plaque/bleeding indices) are small in absolute terms — these are
  index-point differences, not disease outcomes
- Practical: baking soda in toothpaste gives a mild extra cleaning edge and is safe; it is **not** a
  substitute for fluoride. If choosing between "baking soda" and "fluoride", fluoride has the caries
  evidence (T15)
- The best use case: stain removal and mild plaque control in people who already use fluoride

## Strengths and limitations

- Strengths: systematic review restricted to **RCTs**; separates single-use from follow-up designs
  (which is where most reviews fudge the distinction); reports both negative and positive control
  comparisons; bleeding outcomes included alongside plaque
- Limitations: **21 publications / 43 comparisons** is a modest base; abstract reports DiffM but not
  heterogeneity or risk of bias; follow-up durations not stated in the abstract; "positive control"
  composition varies, so the comparison is heterogeneous; no caries endpoint
- The honest headline is the negative one: **no confirmed advantage over established actives in
  sustained use**

## Consistency with other evidence

- Consistent with T80 (in situ: fluoride + baking soda raised plaque fluoride and reduced
  demineralisation vs placebo — but **the difference versus fluoride alone was not significant**).
  Both studies find baking soda helps versus nothing, not versus fluoride
- Consistent with T15 (fluoride toothpaste is the caries-prevention backbone)
- Cross-link T71 (chemical stain removal): baking soda's benefit is largely mechanical/abrasive
  cleaning, which is why single-brushing studies show it and long-term studies attenuate it
